# Operator Experience & Naming Reset

**Status:** Proposed direction; no application implementation is authorized by this document.  
**Working product name:** **TBD** — “Harborline” is retired as a naming direction, but remains in the existing code until a replacement is selected and legally screened.

## The problem this reset solves

The current product demonstration credibly shows three governed communication workflows. It proves that a reorder reminder, allocation alert, and policy-gated notice can each follow a visible sequence of trigger, control, human decision, exception, and audit record.

That is necessary, but it is not yet sufficient for a prospective distributor leader to feel what it would be like to own the product. The first screen currently asks the buyer to inspect three workflow cards. A stronger product experience should place the buyer in an accountable operating role: they should see the day’s coverage risks, the decisions that cannot wait, the people responsible, and the evidence that the operation is under control.

> **North-star experience:** “I am not being shown a message tool. I am sitting at the operating desk where my team protects account coverage, makes the right calls, and knows what happened next.”

The reset does **not** mean building a generic executive dashboard, a CRM, an ERP, or an autonomous sales agent. It means creating a role-shaped operating experience that makes the value of the governed workflow system felt before a buyer opens any individual workflow.

## The product impact to make visible

The product must never invent revenue, cost savings, compliance approval, or realized customer outcomes. It can, however, visibly demonstrate the operating improvements it is designed to create.

| Operating impact | What the buyer should see in the demo | What must remain truthful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Coverage is less dependent on memory** | A live-looking but fictional daily coverage brief shows account moments that have been detected, categorized, and assigned. | The demo may show fictional counts and scenarios; it must not claim revenue recovered. |
| **People know which decisions need them** | A named owner’s queue differentiates a prepared draft, approval request, policy gate, and human exception. | The system does not decide price, allocation, terms, or exceptions. |
| **A team can see work moving without replacing the rep** | A concise operational timeline shows the handoff from signal to preparation to human action to exception. | All delivery remains simulated until a real client implementation authorizes a channel. |
| **Leadership can trust the operation without living in spreadsheets** | An oversight view shows coverage posture, decisions waiting, control gates, and unresolved exception ownership. | This is operating visibility—not a claim of guaranteed performance or compliance. |
| **Existing systems become easier to act on** | A clear source-of-signal label connects each workflow to the distributor’s eventual ERP, CRM, order system, or approved data feed. | The present demo contains no real integration and must say so. |

## The experience to build next

### 1. Start with the operator’s day, not a Workflow Center

The opening should become a **Daily Operating Brief** for a defined persona—initially a distributor sales manager or sales-operations lead. It is not a generic dashboard full of charts. It is a sharply prioritized picture of what the team needs to protect today.

The buyer should immediately understand four things:

1. What account moments require attention now.
2. Which moments are safely prepared versus blocked by a human decision or policy gate.
3. Which named person owns the next action.
4. Which customer conversations require a person rather than automation.

The three existing workflow types should appear as meaningful examples inside this operating brief, not as the entire proposition.

### 2. Make the consequence chain visible

Each workflow detail should answer more than “what message can I send?” It should make the operating consequence legible:

```text
Source signal → account coverage at stake → permitted action → accountable person
→ customer response / exception → recorded outcome → supervisor visibility
```

For a Reorder Reminder, the consequence is an account may be falling outside normal coverage. For Allocation, the consequence is scarce inventory needs responsible, relationship-aware handling. For Policy-Gated Notice, the consequence is that a sensitive communication requires a correct audience and accountable approval.

### 3. Show the same day from more than one seat

The demo should deliberately change perspective without pretending to support a full multi-tenant product yet.

| Seat in the system | What their first screen should answer | Existing demo asset to reuse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sales manager / operations lead** | “Where is team coverage at risk, and where is a human decision holding work?” | Workflow Center, owner labels, audit evidence. |
| **Account owner / rep** | “What do I need to decide or respond to for my accounts today?” | Draft/approval mode, account context, exception handoff. |
| **Portfolio or allocation specialist** | “Which qualified accounts are interested, and what commercial judgment is mine to make?” | Allocation Alert, Marcus handoff. |
| **Sales operations / policy owner** | “Which notice is blocked, why, and who reviewed its audience and terms reference?” | Policy-Gated Notice, Alicia handoff and audit. |

### 4. End with operating memory, not merely an audit list

The current Audit & Policy surface is strong, but it should be framed as **Operating Memory**. The message is not “we store logs.” The message is: “Tomorrow, the team can understand why this customer was contacted, who authorized it, what was excluded, what the customer asked, and who owns the next decision.”

## The revised premium demo story

| Moment | What the buyer experiences | The feeling to create |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Open the Daily Operating Brief** | A fictional distributor day begins with a small number of concrete account moments, named owners, and controls requiring attention. | “This helps me run the day; it is not another place to enter data.” |
| **2. See the coverage posture** | The manager sees work safely prepared, decisions waiting, and human exceptions—not vanity metrics. | “I can see where the team may lose the thread.” |
| **3. Enter one account moment** | The buyer opens Reorder, Allocation, or Policy Notice and sees source signal, account context, permitted message, and control boundary. | “This system knows the difference between help and overreach.” |
| **4. Make or assign the human decision** | A rep approves, a specialist reviews, or operations clears a policy gate. | “It helps people move faster without taking away responsibility.” |
| **5. Observe the exception** | A non-routine buyer request visibly stops automated progression and goes to the right owner. | “It protects the relationship when the situation gets sensitive.” |
| **6. Return to the operating picture** | The day’s status changes, and the evidence remains available. | “I can manage the operation, not chase messages across tools.” |

## Product surfaces to add to the **demo**

These are experience surfaces, not a request to build a full CRM or ERP.

| Priority | Surface | Purpose | Explicit non-goal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | **Daily Operating Brief** | Put the buyer in the manager/operations seat; show the day’s priorities, ownership, gates, and exceptions. | Not an executive analytics dashboard. |
| **2** | **My Decisions / Team Decisions** | Let each role see the small number of items they can unblock today. | Not a generic task manager. |
| **3** | **Account Moment Timeline** | Connect source signal, action, exception, and follow-up outcome around one account. | Not a full customer-history CRM. |
| **4** | **Coverage & Control Overview** | Show whether account coverage is prepared, waiting, blocked, or handed to a person. | Not predictive revenue reporting or claimed ROI. |
| **5** | **Operating Memory** | Reframe audit evidence as team memory and accountable continuity. | Not legal-compliance certification. |

## Naming reset

The current name should not be replaced impulsively. A name has to support the intended category: a premium, human-governed operating layer that helps distributors act on account moments with confidence.

The replacement should be short, ownable, modern, and not so alcohol-specific that it limits the product to a gimmick. It should evoke **clarity, movement, accountable action, and human stewardship**—not a waterfront, a chatbot, an inventory system, or a generic analytics company.

| Naming criterion | What to seek | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Role** | A name that feels like a trusted operating companion or decision environment. | A name that sounds like a consulting firm or a generic CRM. |
| **Energy** | Calm authority, motion, and precision. | Aggressive automation, surveillance, or compliance theater. |
| **Category fit** | Works credibly with distributors while remaining expandable. | Obvious wine/spirits puns, “AI” suffixes, and forced SaaS language. |
| **Practicality** | Distinctive enough for a later domain and trademark screen. | Treating a first idea as cleared without a formal availability review. |

The name-selection process should generate several genuinely different candidate families, select a direction based on the desired product feeling, and only then perform a domain and trademark review. Until that decision, use **“the product”** or **“TBD”** in newly written strategy work rather than deepening the old name.

## Phased upgrade plan

### Phase A — Reframe the premium demo

Create the Daily Operating Brief, role-shaped decision queues, visible coverage/control posture, a cross-workflow account-moment timeline, and a stronger seller story. Keep all data fictional and explicitly simulated. The objective is to make a buyer feel the system’s operating impact in the first sixty seconds.

### Phase B — Build the first real operating core

Build a secure customer workspace around one selected workflow. Introduce real user roles, a database for accounts/workflows/approvals/exceptions/audit events, a controlled data import or approved source feed, and draft-first or rep-approved human action. This is the first genuine product—not a demo account.

### Phase C — Connect to the client’s existing world

Add one approved integration path: a source system provides an event and context, while Harborline returns an action or exception record. Do not replace the ERP, CRM, ordering portal, or communication channel. The first integration must be chosen from a real pilot’s workflow evidence.

### Phase D — Add narrow AI assistance only where it earns trust

Use an LLM to help summarize inbound questions, classify exceptions, or propose clearly marked drafts from approved facts and templates. Keep eligibility, price, terms, allocation, policy gates, delivery authorization, and final human decisions deterministic and reviewable.

## The immediate decision

Before any UI rebuild, choose the intended first seat for the stronger demo:

> **Should the buyer first experience the product as a Sales Manager / Operations Lead protecting team coverage, or as a Rep deciding what to do for their accounts today?**

The recommended starting point is **Sales Manager / Operations Lead** because it lets the partner demonstrate both business impact and human-centered execution. The rep experience then becomes the natural second act of the same story.
